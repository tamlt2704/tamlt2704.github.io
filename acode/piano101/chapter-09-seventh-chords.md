# Chapter 9: Seventh Chords — Songs With Richer Harmony

## The Problem

Your chords sound fine. C major, F major, G major — they work. But after weeks of playing them, they sound... plain. Like eating the same meal every day. Nutritious but boring. You hear jazz pianists, singer-songwriters, R&B artists — their chords sound lush, complex, colorful. Yours sound like a children's song.

Maya plays a C major chord. Then she adds one more note on top — B. The chord transforms. It's still C, still major, but now it sounds sophisticated. Warm. Like the difference between a bare lightbulb and a lamp with a warm shade.

"That's a seventh chord. Same three notes you already know, plus one more. Four notes instead of three. It's the easiest upgrade you'll ever make."

## Why Triads Sound "Plain"

A triad (three notes) is stable. Complete. Resolved. That's its strength — and its limitation. It doesn't pull you anywhere. It just sits there.

A seventh chord (four notes) has tension built in. It wants to move somewhere. It creates forward motion. That's why jazz, R&B, gospel, and sophisticated pop use seventh chords — they keep the music flowing.

## Building Seventh Chords

A seventh chord = a triad + one more note stacked on top (another third above the fifth).

You already know:
- C major triad: C - E - G (root, third, fifth)

Add one more third above G:
- C major seventh: C - E - G - **B** (root, third, fifth, seventh)

The "seventh" is the note that's 7 scale steps above the root (C-D-E-F-G-A-**B** = 7 steps).

## The Three Essential Seventh Chords

### 1. C Major 7 (Cmaj7)

```
Notes:     C    E    G    B
Fingers:   1    2    3    5
           │    │    │    │
           ▼    ▼    ▼    ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
   ▲      ▲      ▲      ▲
   1      2      3      5
```

**C - E - G - B, fingers 1-2-3-5.**

Play it. It sounds dreamy, floating, sophisticated. This is the sound of bossa nova, smooth jazz, and lo-fi beats. It's "home" but with a shimmer.

**Note the fingering:** 1-2-3-5 (not 1-2-4-5). Your ring finger (4) skips this one. Finger 3 on G and finger 5 on B gives a comfortable stretch.

### 2. D Minor 7 (Dm7)

```
Notes:     D    F    A    C
Fingers:   1    2    3    5
           │    │    │    │
           ▼    ▼    ▼    ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
        ▲      ▲      ▲      ▲
        1      2      3      5
```

**D - F - A - C, fingers 1-2-3-5.**

A minor triad (D-F-A) plus C on top. It sounds melancholy but smooth — like rain on a window. Gentle sadness with sophistication.

### 3. G Dominant 7 (G7)

```
Notes:     G    B    D    F
Fingers:   1    2    3    5
           │    │    │    │
           ▼    ▼    ▼    ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ G│ A│ B│ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
   ▲      ▲      ▲      ▲
   1      2      3      5
```

**G - B - D - F, fingers 1-2-3-5.**

This is the most important seventh chord in all of Western music. The **dominant seventh**. It sounds tense, urgent, like it absolutely MUST resolve to C. Play G7, then play Cmaj7. Hear that relief? That pull-and-release is the engine of harmony.

**The difference:** Cmaj7 has a major seventh (B is 11 half steps above G... wait, let me reframe). In G7, the seventh (F) is one half step lower than it would be in Gmaj7 (which would have F♯). That lowered seventh creates the tension.

## The ii-V-I Progression: The Sound of Jazz

Play these three chords in sequence:

```
Dm7  →  G7  →  Cmaj7
(ii)    (V)    (I)
```

```
Dm7:     D-F-A-C     (fingers 1-2-3-5)
G7:      G-B-D-F     (fingers 1-2-3-5)
Cmaj7:   C-E-G-B     (fingers 1-2-3-5)
```

Play each chord for 4 beats. Listen to the flow: tension building (Dm7), maximum tension (G7), resolution (Cmaj7).

This is the **ii-V-I** (two-five-one) progression. It's the most common chord progression in jazz. Every jazz standard uses it — many of them are built almost entirely from ii-V-I's in different keys.

Maya: "If you can play ii-V-I smoothly, you can fake your way through half the jazz repertoire. I'm not even joking."

## Chord Inversions: Same Notes, Different Order

So far, every chord has been in **root position** — the root note (the letter name) is on the bottom. But chords don't have to be played this way.

**C major in three positions:**

```
Root position:    C - E - G     (C on bottom)
First inversion:  E - G - C     (E on bottom, C moved to top)
Second inversion: G - C - E     (G on bottom)
```

These are the SAME chord — same three notes — just rearranged. They sound slightly different (different "color") but they're all C major.

**Why inversions matter:** When you switch between chords, inversions let you move your hand less. Instead of jumping across the keyboard, you find a version of the next chord that's close to where you already are.

### C Major Inversions on the Keyboard

```
Root position (C-E-G):          1st inversion (E-G-C):
Fingers: 1  3  5                Fingers: 1  2  5
   ▼  ▼  ▼                        ▼  ▼     ▼
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐              ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │ C│  │ E│  │ G│              │ E│  │ G│  │  │ C│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘              └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘

2nd inversion (G-C-E):
Fingers: 1  3  5
   ▼     ▼  ▼
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │ G│  │  │ C│  │ E│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
```

**Slash chord notation:** Inversions are written with a slash:
- C/E = C major with E in the bass (first inversion)
- C/G = C major with G in the bass (second inversion)

## Voice Leading: The Art of Minimal Movement

**Voice leading** means moving between chords with the smallest possible finger movements. Instead of jumping your whole hand to a new position, you keep common notes and move the others by the smallest interval.

**Example: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7 with voice leading**

Without voice leading (root position, big jumps):
```
Dm7:    D - F - A - C     (hand position 1)
G7:     G - B - D - F     (jump to new position)
Cmaj7:  C - E - G - B     (jump again)
```

With voice leading (minimal movement):
```
Dm7:    D - F - A - C     (start here)
G7:     D - F - G - B     (D stays, F stays, A→G, C→B)
Cmaj7:  C - E - G - B     (D→C, F→E, G stays, B stays)
```

Wait — that G7 doesn't look like G-B-D-F. It's the same notes rearranged: G is there, B is there, D is there, F is there. It's G7 in a different inversion.

**The principle:** When two chords share notes, keep those notes in place. Move everything else by the smallest possible distance (usually a half step or whole step).

This is what makes professional pianists sound smooth — their chords flow into each other instead of jumping.

## Exercise 2: The ii-V-I in Practice

Play the ii-V-I progression slowly, 4 beats per chord:

```
| Dm7 (4 beats) | G7 (4 beats) | Cmaj7 (4 beats) | Cmaj7 (4 beats) |
```

Repeat 4 times. Then try it with a simple right-hand melody on top:

```
Right hand:  D  E  F  G  │  G  A  B  C  │  C─────────────
```

The melody walks up while the chords progress. Simple but effective.

## Exercise 3: Inversions Practice

Play C major in all three positions:

```
Root:      C - E - G    (1-3-5)
1st inv:   E - G - C    (1-2-5)
2nd inv:   G - C - E    (1-3-5)
```

Then do the same for F major and G major:

```
F root:    F - A - C    (1-3-5)
F 1st:     A - C - F    (1-2-5)
F 2nd:     C - F - A    (1-3-5)

G root:    G - B - D    (1-3-5)
G 1st:     B - D - G    (1-2-5)
G 2nd:     D - G - B    (1-3-5)
```

## Exercise 4: Voice-Led Progression

Play C-F-G-C using inversions so your hand barely moves:

```
C root:     C - E - G       (start here)
F 2nd inv:  C - F - A       (C stays, E→F, G→A — small moves!)
G 1st inv:  B - D - G       (C→B, F→D, A→G)
C root:     C - E - G       (B→C, D→E, G stays)
```

Compare this to jumping between root positions. The voice-led version sounds smoother and is physically easier.

## Exercise 5: A Jazz-Flavored Song

Put it all together. Left hand plays seventh chords, right hand plays a simple melody:

```
Progression: | Dm7      | G7       | Cmaj7    | Cmaj7    |
Left hand:   | D-F-A-C  | G-B-D-F  | C-E-G-B  | C-E-G-B  |
Right hand:  | D E F A  | G A B D  | C E G E  | C─────── |
Fingers RH:  | 1 2 3 5  | 1 2 3 5  | 1 3 5 3  | 1        |
```

Play this loop 4 times. It sounds like a jazz intro. Add it to your repertoire — it's a great warm-up that sounds impressive.

## What You Learned

- **Seventh chords** — triads plus one more note (four notes total)
- **Cmaj7** — C-E-G-B (dreamy, floating home)
- **Dm7** — D-F-A-C (gentle tension)
- **G7** — G-B-D-F (strong tension, wants to resolve)
- **The ii-V-I** — Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 (the backbone of jazz)
- **Inversions** — same notes in different order (C, C/E, C/G)
- **Voice leading** — move as little as possible between chords
