## The Story

This is a series about learning piano — but not the kind where you memorize "Every Good Boy Does Fine" and stare at sheet music for months before touching a key.

You're **you** — someone who's always wanted to play piano but never found the right entry point. Maybe you tried once and got buried in theory. Maybe you watched someone play and thought "I could never do that." Maybe you just bought a keyboard and it's sitting there, judging you.

Your friend **Maya**, a gigging jazz pianist, drops by your place. She sees the keyboard.

"You know what most piano teachers get wrong? They start with reading music. That's like learning to read before you can speak. Kids learn to talk first — by listening, copying, experimenting. Music works the same way. Let's get you playing something today. Theory comes when you need it."

Over the next 15 chapters, you'll go from pressing your first key to playing songs, understanding chords, reading basic notation, and improvising. Every concept arrives because you need it — not because a curriculum says so.

You'll hit wrong notes. Your left hand will refuse to cooperate with your right. Your timing will drift. A chord will sound muddy and you won't know why. Each frustration teaches you something about how music actually works.

By the end, you'll be able to sit down at any piano and play. Not concert-level — but real music that sounds good and feels good.

## How to Read This

Every chapter follows the same loop:

1. You want to play something specific
2. You discover what's missing — a technique, a concept, a pattern
3. You learn the minimum theory needed to unlock it
4. You practice it with concrete exercises
5. You play something that sounds like actual music

No theory shows up before you need it. You won't learn key signatures until a song sounds wrong without sharps. You won't touch scales until your fingers need a path they can follow without thinking.

The sound comes first. The explanation follows.

## What You Need

### A Keyboard or Piano

Any of these work:

| Type | Keys | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acoustic piano | 88 | Best feel and sound | Expensive, heavy, needs tuning |
| Digital piano | 88 | Weighted keys, headphone jack | Mid-price, still large |
| 61-key keyboard | 61 | Affordable, portable | Lighter keys, fewer octaves |
| 49-key MIDI controller | 49 | Cheapest, compact | No built-in sound, needs computer |

**Minimum requirement:** 49 keys (4 octaves). You need at least this many to play with both hands comfortably.

**Ideal:** 88 or 76 weighted keys. Weighted keys build finger strength and feel like a real piano. But don't let "ideal" stop you — a $50 keyboard is infinitely better than no keyboard.

### Headphones (Optional but Recommended)

You'll practice more if you're not self-conscious about neighbors hearing your mistakes. Any headphones with a 3.5mm or 6.3mm jack work.

### A Way to Sit

Your forearms should be roughly parallel to the floor when your fingers rest on the keys. A proper piano bench is ideal, but a dining chair at the right height works. Don't play standing — your wrists will hate you within a week.

### No Sheet Music (Yet)

We won't read traditional notation until Chapter 8. Everything before that uses letter names (C, D, E...), finger numbers (1-5), and simple diagrams. If you can read this text, you can follow along.

### Quick Check

Sit at your keyboard. Press any white key. Did it make a sound? You're ready.

## The Roadmap

| Ch | What You Want to Play | What You Learn |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A single melody with one hand | Finding notes, Middle C, the white key pattern |
| 2 | That melody with better flow | Finger numbers, hand position, basic technique |
| 3 | Something that sounds "full" | Your first chords — C, F, G |
| 4 | A song with both hands | Left hand patterns, hand independence |
| 5 | Songs in different moods | Major vs minor, the emotional palette |
| 6 | Songs that move smoothly | Scales as finger highways, muscle memory |
| 7 | Songs with rhythm and groove | Time signatures, counting, syncopation |
| 8 | Songs from sheet music | Reading notation — treble clef, bass clef |
| 9 | Songs with richer harmony | Seventh chords, inversions, voice leading |
| 10 | Songs that flow between sections | Song structure, transitions, dynamics |
| 11 | Songs with expression | Pedal technique, touch, phrasing |
| 12 | Songs in any key | Transposition, the number system |
| 13 | Your own arrangements | Chord charts, lead sheets, arranging |
| 14 | Improvisation | Pentatonic scale, blues, playing by ear |
| 15 | Performing for others | Practice routines, stage fright, what's next |

## One Rule

**Play every day.** Ten minutes of daily practice beats two hours on Saturday. Your fingers learn through repetition, and they forget through absence. Even on busy days — sit down, play something for five minutes, get up. The streak matters more than the session length.

Maya's advice: "You don't practice until you get it right. You practice until you can't get it wrong."

Let's find Middle C.
