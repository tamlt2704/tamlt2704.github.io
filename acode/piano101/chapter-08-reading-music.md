## The Problem

You can play songs — but only songs you've memorized from letter names and finger numbers. There are thousands of pieces you'd love to play, but they're written in a language you can't read: sheet music. Those five lines, those little black dots, those mysterious symbols — they look like a secret code.

Maya pulls out a piece of sheet music. "It IS a code. But it's a simple one. Five lines. Dots go up, the pitch goes up. Dots go down, the pitch goes down. The shape of the dot tells you how long to hold it. That's basically it."

Professor Tanaka leans in. "She's oversimplifying. But she's not wrong. Let me show you the system."

## Why Learn to Read

You've gotten this far without reading music. Why bother now?

- **Access:** Thousands of songs are available as sheet music. Without reading, you're limited to what you can learn by ear or from tutorials.
- **Independence:** You can learn any new piece on your own, without someone showing you the notes.
- **Communication:** If you ever play with other musicians, sheet music is the common language.
- **Memory aid:** Even if you memorize a piece, the sheet music is there when you forget.

You don't need to be a fast sight-reader. Even slow reading — decoding one note at a time — opens up a world of music.

## The Staff: Five Lines

Music is written on a **staff** — five horizontal lines with four spaces between them:

```
─────────────────────────────────────  ← Line 5
                                       ← Space 4
─────────────────────────────────────  ← Line 4
                                       ← Space 3
─────────────────────────────────────  ← Line 3
                                       ← Space 2
─────────────────────────────────────  ← Line 2
                                       ← Space 1
─────────────────────────────────────  ← Line 1
```

Notes sit ON lines (the line goes through the middle of the note) or IN spaces (the note fills the gap between two lines). Higher position = higher pitch.

## The Treble Clef (Right Hand)

The treble clef (𝄞) marks the staff for higher notes — typically your right hand. It looks like a fancy cursive G, and it wraps around the second line from the bottom, telling you that line = G.

**Lines of the treble clef (bottom to top): E - G - B - D - F**

Memory trick: **E**very **G**ood **B**oy **D**oes **F**ine

**Spaces of the treble clef (bottom to top): F - A - C - E**

Memory trick: The spaces spell **FACE**

```
Treble Clef Staff:

                                    F ─── Line 5
                                 E      Space 4
                              D ─── Line 4
                           C      Space 3
                        B ─── Line 3
                     A      Space 2
                  G ─── Line 2
               F      Space 1
            E ─── Line 1
```

**Quick reference — treble clef notes on the keyboard:**

```
Line/Space:  E  F  G  A  B  C  D  E  F
Position:    L1 S1 L2 S2 L3 S3 L4 S4 L5
             │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
             ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██     ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██     ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│ D│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
            ↑                          ↑
         Treble E                   Treble F
         (Line 1)                   (Line 5)
```

The treble clef covers roughly the notes from E (above Middle C area) up to F and beyond.

## The Bass Clef (Left Hand)

The bass clef (𝄢) marks the staff for lower notes — typically your left hand. It looks like a backwards C with two dots, and it identifies the fourth line as F.

**Lines of the bass clef (bottom to top): G - B - D - F - A**

Memory trick: **G**ood **B**oys **D**o **F**ine **A**lways

**Spaces of the bass clef (bottom to top): A - C - E - G**

Memory trick: **A**ll **C**ows **E**at **G**rass

```
Bass Clef Staff:

                                    A ─── Line 5
                                 G      Space 4
                              F ─── Line 4
                           E      Space 3
                        D ─── Line 3
                     C      Space 2
                  B ─── Line 2
               A      Space 1
            G ─── Line 1
```

## Middle C: The Bridge

Middle C doesn't live on either staff — it sits on a short extra line called a **ledger line** between the two staves:

```
Treble Staff:
─────────────────────────  (Line 1 of treble = E)

── ● ──  ← Middle C (one ledger line BELOW the treble staff)


── ● ──  ← Middle C (one ledger line ABOVE the bass staff)

─────────────────────────  (Line 5 of bass = A)
Bass Staff:
```

Middle C is the same note whether written below the treble staff or above the bass staff. It's the meeting point between your two hands.

## The Grand Staff

Piano music uses both staves together, connected by a bracket. This is called the **grand staff**:

```
        ┌─ Treble Clef (right hand) ─────────────────────
        │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
        │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
        │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  ┌─┐   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  │ │   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  │ │   │
  │ │   │         ── ● ──  (Middle C - ledger line)
  │ │   │
  │ │   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  │ │   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  │ │   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  │ │   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
  └─┘   └─ Bass Clef (left hand) ───────────────────────
              ─────────────────────────────────────────────
```

Right hand reads the top staff. Left hand reads the bottom staff. You read both simultaneously — which is why sight-reading takes practice.

## Reading Rhythm: Note Shapes

You learned note values in Chapter 7. Here's how they look on paper:

```
Whole note:      ○         (open oval, no stem)     = 4 beats
Half note:       𝅗𝅥         (open oval + stem)       = 2 beats
Quarter note:    ♩         (filled oval + stem)     = 1 beat
Eighth note:     ♪         (filled + stem + flag)   = ½ beat
Two eighths:     ♫         (filled + beam)          = ½ + ½ beat
```

**Rests** (silence) have their own symbols:

```
Whole rest:      ▬  (hangs from line 4)   = 4 beats of silence
Half rest:       ▄  (sits on line 3)      = 2 beats of silence
Quarter rest:    𝄾  (zigzag shape)        = 1 beat of silence
Eighth rest:     𝄿  (small flag shape)    = ½ beat of silence
```

## Exercise 1: Treble Clef Note Reading

Name these notes as fast as you can. Use the mnemonics:

```
Lines (bottom to top):  E - G - B - D - F  ("Every Good Boy Does Fine")
Spaces (bottom to top): F - A - C - E      ("FACE")
```

**Drill:** Point to random lines and spaces on a treble staff and name the note. Do 20 in a row. Time yourself. Repeat daily until you can name any note in under 2 seconds.

## Exercise 3: Note-to-Key Connection

The goal isn't just naming notes — it's connecting what you see on paper to what your fingers play. Practice this chain:

```
See note on staff → Name it → Find it on keyboard → Play it
```

Start with just the treble clef. Read these notes one at a time and play them:

```
Treble clef sequence 1:  E  G  B  D  F  (lines, going up)
Treble clef sequence 2:  F  A  C  E      (spaces, going up)
Treble clef sequence 3:  C  D  E  F  G   (Middle C up to G — stepwise)
```

Then bass clef:

```
Bass clef sequence 1:  G  B  D  F  A  (lines, going up)
Bass clef sequence 2:  A  C  E  G      (spaces, going up)
Bass clef sequence 3:  C  B  A  G  F   (Middle C down to F — stepwise)
```

## Exercise 4: Sight-Reading a Simple Piece

Here's "Mary Had a Little Lamb" mapped to staff positions:

```
Starting on E (first space of treble clef):

E(Space 1) → D(below Line 1) → C(ledger line) → D(below Line 1)
E(Space 1) → E(Space 1) → E(Space 1, half note)
D(below Line 1) → D → D(half note)
E(Space 1) → G(Line 2) → G(Line 2, half note)
```

The key insight: notes that are close together on the staff are close together on the keyboard. Steps on the staff = steps on the keys. Skips on the staff = skips on the keys.

## Exercise 5: Reading by Interval (Not Just Note Names)

Fast readers don't name every note. They read **intervals** — the distance between notes:

```
Same line/space to same line/space = same note (repeat)
Line to next space (or space to next line) = step up
Space to next line (or line to next space) = step down
Line to next line (skipping a space) = skip up (a third)
Space to next space (skipping a line) = skip down (a third)
```

**Practice:** Look at two consecutive notes. Don't name them — just identify: step up, step down, skip up, skip down, or repeat. Then play the interval.

This is faster than naming every note individually, and it's how experienced readers actually process music.

## Tips for Beginning Sight-Readers

Maya's practical advice:

"Don't try to read fast. Read ahead by one note — look at the next note while you're playing the current one. That's the whole secret of sight-reading. Your eyes stay ahead of your fingers."

**More tips:**
1. **Start with just the right hand.** Ignore the bass clef for now.
2. **Look for patterns.** Scales going up? You already know how those feel. Repeated notes? Easy.
3. **Don't stop for mistakes.** Keep the rhythm going. A wrong note in time is better than a right note late.
4. **Use landmark notes.** You don't need to count from the bottom every time. Know where G (line 2) and C (space 3) are, and count from there.
5. **Practice a little every day.** Five minutes of sight-reading daily beats an hour once a week.

## What You Learned

- **The staff** — 5 lines and 4 spaces, higher position = higher pitch
- **Treble clef** — right hand; lines E-G-B-D-F, spaces F-A-C-E
- **Bass clef** — left hand; lines G-B-D-F-A, spaces A-C-E-G
- **Middle C** — one ledger line below treble or above bass
- **The grand staff** — both staves together for piano
- **Note shapes** — whole, half, quarter, eighth (duration from shape)
- **Reading by interval** — step, skip, or repeat (faster than naming)
