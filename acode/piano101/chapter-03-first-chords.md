# Chapter 3: First Chords — Something That Sounds Full

## The Problem

You can play melodies. One note at a time, smooth and even. But it sounds thin. Like singing without accompaniment. Like a voice in an empty room.

Maya plays a single melody line — "Twinkle Twinkle" — with one finger. Then she plays it again, but this time her left hand drops three notes at once underneath. The room fills up. It sounds like music.

"That's a chord. Three or more notes played at the same time. One note is a melody. Three notes together are harmony. Harmony is what makes music sound full."

## What Is a Chord?

A chord is simply three or more notes pressed simultaneously. Your hand is already shaped for this — remember the curved position from Chapter 2? Three fingers pressing three keys at once.

The most basic chord is a **triad** — three notes stacked in a specific pattern. Today you'll learn three triads that unlock hundreds of songs.

## The C Major Chord

Place your right hand in C position. Now press these three keys at the same time:

```
Finger:    1         3         5
Note:      C         E         G
           │         │         │
           ▼         ▼         ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
   ▲         ▲         ▲
   1         3         5
```

**C - E - G, fingers 1 - 3 - 5.**

Press all three at exactly the same time. Hold them. Listen.

That's C major. Bright. Stable. Resolved. It sounds like "home."

**The pattern:** You're skipping one white key between each note. C (skip D) E (skip F) G. This skip pattern — note, skip, note, skip, note — is how every major triad is built.

## The F Major Chord

Move your hand up so your thumb is on F:

```
Finger:    1         3         5
Note:      F         A         C
           │         │         │
           ▼         ▼         ▼
    ██  ██  ██     ██  ██
    ██  ██  ██     ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ F│ G│ A│ B│ C│ D│ E│ F│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
   ▲         ▲         ▲
   1         3         5
```

**F - A - C, fingers 1 - 3 - 5.**

Same shape. Same skip pattern. Different starting note. F (skip G) A (skip B) C.

Play it. It sounds like it wants to go somewhere — like a question that needs an answer.

## The G Major Chord

Move your hand so your thumb is on G:

```
Finger:    1         3         5
Note:      G         B         D
           │         │         │
           ▼         ▼         ▼
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
    ██  ██     ██  ██  ██
  ┌──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┬──┐
  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │  │
  │ G│ A│ B│ C│ D│ E│ F│ G│
  └──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┴──┘
   ▲         ▲         ▲
   1         3         5
```

**G - B - D, fingers 1 - 3 - 5.**

Same shape again. G (skip A) B (skip C) D.

Play it. This one sounds tense — like it's pulling you back toward C. It wants to resolve.

## The Three-Chord Trick

Now play them in sequence:

```
C major → F major → G major → C major
(C-E-G)   (F-A-C)   (G-B-D)   (C-E-G)
```

Play each chord, hold it for 4 beats, then move to the next.

Hear that? It sounds like a complete musical thought. A journey that leaves home, goes somewhere, builds tension, and comes back. That's not an accident.

Maya grins. "Those three chords — C, F, and G — are the I, IV, and V chords in the key of C. Roman numerals. Don't worry about why yet. Just know that these three chords are the backbone of thousands of songs. Rock, pop, country, folk, hymns — most of them are built on I-IV-V."

## Why These Three Work Together

Professor Tanaka adjusts his glasses. "May I?"

Here's the intuitive version: The key of C major uses only white keys. The C chord (C-E-G), F chord (F-A-C), and G chord (G-B-D) together contain every single white key note:

```
C chord:  C . E . G . .
F chord:  . . . F . A C
G chord:  . . G . . B D
─────────────────────────
Together: C D E F G A B  ← all 7 notes covered
```

These three chords "own" the entire key. Any melody using white keys will fit over at least one of these chords. That's why they work for so many songs.

"Thank you, Professor," Maya says. "Now forget the theory and just listen. Does it sound good? Then it's right."

## Exercise 1: Chord Switching

Play each chord 4 times (4 beats each), then switch:

```
| C  C  C  C | F  F  F  F | G  G  G  G | C  C  C  C |
```

**Focus on:**
- All three notes sounding at exactly the same time (no rolling)
- Keeping the curved hand shape as you move
- Moving your whole hand as a unit — don't let fingers drift
- Maintaining even volume across all three notes

Repeat until the switches feel smooth. The jump from C to F is small (thumb moves up 3 keys). The jump from F to G is tiny (thumb moves up 1 key). The jump from G back to C is the biggest — your thumb drops back 4 keys.

**The physical motion:** Lift your hand slightly (just enough to clear the keys), shift to the new position, and drop. Don't slide — lift, move, drop. Keep the hand shape frozen during the move, like picking up a cup and setting it down somewhere else.

**Common problems:**

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Notes don't sound together | Fingers landing at different times | Practice the "drop" — all fingers land as one unit |
| Gap between chords | Lifting too early, moving too slowly | Minimize air time — quick lift, quick move |
| Wrong notes after switch | Not looking ahead | Eyes find the next position while current chord is still sounding |
| Hand shape collapses | Tension during movement | Stay relaxed, keep the curved shape |

## Exercise 2: Chord Patterns

Try these common patterns. Each letter = 4 beats:

```
Pattern 1:  C  F  G  C       (the basic I-IV-V-I)
Pattern 2:  C  G  F  C       (I-V-IV-I — "Wild Thing")
Pattern 3:  C  F  C  G       (I-IV-I-V — verse pattern)
Pattern 4:  C  C  F  G       (I-I-IV-V — building tension)
```

Play each pattern 4 times through. Notice how different orderings create different feelings, even though you're using the same three chords.

## Exercise 3: Left Hand Chords + Right Hand Melody

Here's where it gets exciting. Your left hand plays chords while your right hand plays a melody.

**Setup:**
- Left hand: Play chords one octave below Middle C (find the C below Middle C)
- Right hand: Play melody starting at Middle C

**"Twinkle Twinkle" with chords:**

```
Right hand:  C  C  G  G  A  A  G     F  F  E  E  D  D  C
Left hand:   C.........              F..........  C.........
             (C-E-G)                  (F-A-C)     (C-E-G)

Right hand:  G  G  F  F  E  E  D     G  G  F  F  E  E  D
Left hand:   C..........  F.......    C..........  G.........
             (C-E-G)      (F-A-C)     (C-E-G)      (G-B-D)

Right hand:  C  C  G  G  A  A  G     F  F  E  E  D  D  C
Left hand:   C.........              F..........  C.........
             (C-E-G)                  (F-A-C)     (C-E-G)
```

Don't worry about playing both hands together yet — that's Chapter 4. For now, practice the left hand chords alone until the switches are smooth. Then practice the right hand melody alone. We'll combine them next chapter.

## Exercise 4: Recognizing Chords by Sound

Play each chord with your eyes closed:

1. Play C major. Listen. This is "home."
2. Play F major. Listen. This is "away" — gentle departure.
3. Play G major. Listen. This is "tension" — wants to go home.
4. Play C major again. Listen. Relief. Resolution.

Now have someone else play one of the three chords (or play one without looking). Can you tell which one it is by sound alone? This ear training will serve you for years.

## The Chord Naming System

You'll see chords written different ways. They all mean the same thing:

| Written As | Means | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| C | C major | C-E-G |
| Cmaj | C major | C-E-G |
| F | F major | F-A-C |
| G | G major | G-B-D |

When you see just a letter — like "C" or "F" — it always means major. Minor chords get a special marking (we'll learn those in Chapter 5).

## What You Learned

- **A chord** — three or more notes played simultaneously
- **The skip pattern** — note, skip one, note, skip one, note = major triad
- **C major** — C-E-G (home, stable, resolved)
- **F major** — F-A-C (departure, movement)
- **G major** — G-B-D (tension, wants to resolve to C)
- **I-IV-V** — the three-chord backbone of thousands of songs
- **Why they work** — together they cover all 7 notes of the key
